Credit where it's due

This is better than most first accounts. Root MFA on and no root access keys are the two checks that matter most, and you have both. What's missing is the entire role half of IAM — which is what this module is about.

The core idea

An identity is who you are. A credential is proof.

They are separate things, and the whole art of AWS security follows from keeping them separate:

The principle

Keep identities stable. Make credentials short-lived.

Your laptop currently does the opposite. One identity (lisakalex) welded to one credential (AKIA2YLI…) that has been valid for 39 days and stays valid forever until you personally delete it. Identity and credential have collapsed into the same object.

That collapse is the problem roles exist to solve.

The landscape

Three kinds of identity

1 — never use it

Root user

Created with the account, tied to the email. Not an IAM user — IAM policies cannot restrict it. You cannot take permissions away from root, ever.

A few things only root can do: close the account, change the support plan, fix a bucket policy that locked everyone out. Otherwise it sits untouched behind MFA.

2 — what you have

IAM user

A named identity carrying its own long-lived credentials: a password for the console, access keys (AKIA…) for the API.

Person-shaped, permanent, and the source of most leaked-credential incidents.

3 — the answer

IAM role

An identity with no credentials at all. No password, no access keys. You assume it and receive temporary credentials that expire.

Usable by humans and machines — which is how servers get permissions without storing secrets.

The uncomfortable part about access keys

That AKIA… key is the identity, as far as AWS is concerned. Anyone holding those two strings is you — not “acting on your behalf,” but indistinguishable from you in CloudTrail, with AdministratorAccess.

MFA does not change this. MFA protects console sign-in. It does not protect API calls made with an access key. Your key works with no second factor, from anywhere on Earth. That is not a misconfiguration — it is how access keys work.

The concept everything rests on

A role is a hat, not a person

Nobody logs in as a role. A role has no password and no access keys. The useful mental image:

The hat

A hat sits on a hook. It isn't anybody. But if you're authorised to wear it, you put it on — and while you're wearing it you have its powers. Take it off and they're gone.

“Putting it on” is an API call: sts:AssumeRole. What comes back is a temporary credential set — an ASIA… key, a secret, and a session token — that expires, typically in an hour.

That last row is the one that matters most. A role can be assumed by a machine — so machines never need stored secrets. Your GitHub Actions workflow and your Kubernetes pod both rely on this. That is what IRSA and OIDC federation are, and we take them apart in Lesson 2.4.

Memorise this

Every role has two policies

A role is defined by two entirely separate documents answering two different questions. Both already exist in your terraform/main.tf:

Policy 1 WHO may wear the hat?

The trust policy. Names the principals allowed to assume this role — an account, a user, a service, or a federated identity.

aws_iam_role.app_role
  .assume_role_policy
Policy 2 WHAT can the wearer do?

The permissions policy. The actions and resources the role grants once assumed — ordinary Allow statements.

aws_iam_role_policy
  .app_policy

Why mixing them up is so dangerous

The two failure modes are asymmetric, and that asymmetry is the whole lesson:

A too-narrow permissions policy fails loudly. Your app throws AccessDenied. You notice in minutes.

A too-broad trust policy fails silently. Nothing breaks. Everything works. You have simply allowed the wrong parties to become your role — and you find out when somebody else does.

That is exactly the flaw sitting in your terraform/main.tf today — the one your own docs/INTERVIEW_PRACTICE.md marked “correct but incomplete.” We read it line by line in Lesson 2.4, and by then you should spot it before I point at it.

The gap

You currently have no hats

# non service-linked roles in the account: $ aws iam list-roles --query 'Roles[?!starts_with(Path,`/aws-service-role/`)]' (empty)

There is no way for you to obtain temporary credentials right now. Every command run in this course so far — every audit, every Cost Explorer query — used that permanent admin key. Closing this gap is the hands-on exercise at the end of the module.

Four questions

Answer in the terminal — reasoning matters more than being right, and “don't know” is genuinely useful information for me. Open a card only after you've committed to an answer.

1. You have MFA on lisakalex. Your access key and secret leak into a public gist. Does MFA stop an attacker using them?

Commit to an answer first — then tell me in the terminal and I'll grade the reasoning, not just the verdict. The relevant material is in “The uncomfortable part about access keys” above.

2. In your own words: what's the practical difference between an IAM user and an IAM role? I want the credential consequence, not a definition.

A definition earns half marks. What I'm listening for is what changes for an attacker who steals each one.

3. A role has two policies — trust and permissions. Which is dangerous to get wrong, and why that one?

Both can be wrong. Only one tells you it's wrong. That's the thread to pull.

4. An EC2 instance needs to read from S3. Option A: an IAM user with keys in a config file on the instance. Option B: attach a role. Give two concrete reasons B is better.

Two distinct reasons — “it's more secure” twice doesn't count. One is about what happens when the instance is compromised; one is about what happens over time when nothing goes wrong at all.
