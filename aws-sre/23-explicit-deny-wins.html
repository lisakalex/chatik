Explicit Deny Wins

How AWS actually decides yes or no. It's three rules in a fixed order, and knowing them explains the most common wasted afternoon in AWS: “I added the permission and it still doesn't work.”

The vocabulary

There are three answers, not two

Everyone assumes AWS answers allowed or denied. It actually distinguishes two different kinds of no, and the difference matters enormously. Run against your own account:

# your user — AdministratorAccess s3:ListAllMyBuckets allowed iam:ListUsers allowed ec2:TerminateInstances allowed # the learning-mfa-demo role — one permission only s3:ListAllMyBuckets allowed iam:ListUsers implicitDeny s3:GetObject implicitDeny # your admin user, with a single explicit Deny added ec2:TerminateInstances explicitDeny s3:ListAllMyBuckets allowed
AnswerMeansHow to fix it
allowedSome policy said Allow, and nothing said Deny—
implicitDenyNobody mentioned it. No policy allows it, none denies itAdd an Allow
explicitDenyA policy said Deny outrightFind and remove the Deny. Adding Allow will not help

Why this distinction saves you hours

If you're getting implicitDeny, adding a permission fixes it. If you're getting explicitDeny, you can add permissions all day and nothing will change — you have to find the Deny and remove it. Knowing which one you're facing tells you where to look.

The mechanism

The decision, in order

Proved on your own account

Your user holds AdministratorAccess — "Action": "*" on "Resource": "*". Adding one Deny statement produced explicitDeny for that action, while everything else stayed allowed.

Administrator is not a trump card. There is no permission level in AWS that overrides an explicit Deny — not admin, not the account owner. Only removing the Deny works.

Reading the JSON

What a policy is made of

{ "Version": "2012-10-17", ← schema version, NOT a date you pick. Always this. "Statement": [{ "Sid": "AllowBucketList", ← optional label, for humans "Effect": "Allow", ← Allow or Deny "Action": ["s3:ListBucket"], ← WHAT verb "Resource": ["arn:aws:s3:::my-bucket"], ← on WHICH thing "Condition": { "Bool": { "aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent": "true" } } ← WHEN }] }

Five fields worth knowing. Version catches people out — it's a fixed schema identifier, not today's date, and 2012-10-17 is the only value you should ever write.

The sixth field, and why it's the important one

There's one more: Principal — who. And whether a policy has it tells you what kind of policy it is.

Where policies live

Policies attach to two different things

Identity-based

Attached to a user or role

“This identity may do X to Y.” No Principal field — the principal is whoever it's attached to.

AdministratorAccess on your user is one. So is the list-buckets-only policy on your demo role.

Resource-based

Attached to the thing itself

“These principals may do X to me.” Has a Principal field, because the resource must say who it's talking about.

S3 bucket policies, KMS key policies, SQS queue policies — and role trust policies, which are just resource-based policies on a role.

That's the answer to something from Lesson 2.1: a trust policy is a resource-based policy. It hangs off the role and names who may assume it. Same JSON shape, Principal included.

Your account has one real resource-based policy already — on the KMS key protecting your Terraform state:

$ aws kms get-key-policy --key-id 85618890-… --policy-name default { "Version": "2012-10-17", "Statement": [{ "Sid": "Enable IAM User Permissions", "Effect": "Allow", "Principal": { "AWS": "arn:aws:iam::739489595894:root" }, "Action": "kms:*", "Resource": "*" }] }

root here does not mean the root user

This trips up almost everybody. In a resource policy, arn:aws:iam::739489595894:root means “the account as a whole” — not the root login.

What the statement actually says is: “defer to IAM.” Anyone in account 739489595894 whose identity-based policy allows a KMS action may perform it. Without this statement the key would be unusable by everyone — including you — and since a key policy is the only way to grant KMS access, you can permanently lock yourself out of a KMS key. It's the classic way to make a bucket's data unrecoverable.

Your S3 state bucket, by contrast, has no bucket policy at all (NoSuchBucketPolicy) — access is decided purely by identity-based policies. That's normal and usually preferable; reach for a bucket policy when you need to grant access to another account, or to deny something account-wide.

The tool

Stop guessing — simulate it

Every output on this page came from the policy simulator, which answers “would this be allowed?” without actually doing it. Read-only, free, and it tells you which of the three answers you'd get:

aws iam simulate-principal-policy \ --policy-source-arn arn:aws:iam::739489595894:role/learning-mfa-demo \ --action-names s3:ListAllMyBuckets iam:ListUsers \ --query 'EvaluationResults[].[EvalActionName,EvalDecision]' --output text

Add --resource-arns to test a specific resource, or --policy-input-list to try a policy you haven't written yet. It's the right first move whenever someone reports “I can't do X” — it distinguishes implicitDeny from explicitDeny in one command, and that tells you whether you're adding a permission or hunting a Deny.
